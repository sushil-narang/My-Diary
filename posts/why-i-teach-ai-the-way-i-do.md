Every semester, a few students walk into my first class expecting walls of
equations. They've been warned by seniors, or by the internet, that Artificial
Intelligence is "all maths." By the end of the term, most of them tell me the
maths was the easy part. The hard part was learning to *think* in terms of
problems and data.

Here's why I teach AI the way I do.

## Start with the problem, not the model

I open every new topic with a question, not a definition:

- How does your phone unlock with your face?
- How does YouTube decide what you watch next?
- How does a chatbot know that "bank" means something different in "river bank"
  than in "bank account"?

Only once a student can articulate the *problem* do we ask what kind of model
might solve it. It sounds obvious, but it reverses the usual order of
instruction — and it changes everything. Students stop memorising algorithms
and start matching tools to situations, which is the actual skill.

## Make the first success small and fast

The fastest way to kill curiosity is a three-week detour into theory before
anyone sees a result. So in week one, my students train a model on their own
data — marks, steps, cricket scores, anything — and see a prediction by the
end of the lab session.

It's a trivial model. It's also *theirs*. That small win carries them through
the harder weeks that follow.

## Show the failure cases loudly

AI is easy to oversell, and I'd rather my students be the ones who can see the
cracks. When we build a model, we spend as much time on where it breaks as on
where it works:

- What data was missing?
- Who is harmed when it's wrong?
- Would you trust this decision if it were made about *you*?

A student who can criticise a model they built is far more valuable — and far
safer — than one who can only run it.

## Maths arrives when it earns its place

Do we cover gradient descent, loss functions, probability? Of course. But by
the time the maths arrives, students already have a working mental picture of
what it's doing. The formula stops being a wall and becomes an explanation.

> Give a student a working model and they'll be curious about the maths.
> Give them the maths first and many will never see the model.

## What I'd tell a new AI educator

1. **Curiosity is the prerequisite, not maths.** Everything else can be taught.
2. **Build before you derive.** Intuition makes theory land.
3. **Normalise failure.** Broken models are the lesson, not a detour from it.
4. **Stay current yourself.** The field moves fast; your students will notice
   if your examples are three years old.

That's the shape of every course I design. The tools change almost every year —
this year it's large language models and retrieval, last year it was diffusion
models — but the shape holds.
