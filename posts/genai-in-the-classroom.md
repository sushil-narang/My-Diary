Two years ago I banned the obvious AI tools from assignments. Last semester I
did the opposite: I told students they could use them for anything, provided
they could defend every line. The results were more interesting than I expected.

## What improved immediately

**Writing quality went up — but so did boredom.**

The first submissions were uniformly polished and completely lifeless. Fifty
essays with the same rhythm, the same "In conclusion," the same three-part
transitions. I could grade faster, and I enjoyed it less.

The fix was simple: I banned *first drafts* from being generated. Students
could use tools for research, structure, critique and rewriting — but the
first pass had to be their own words. Quality stayed high; voice came back.

**Weaker students gained the most.**

The students who were struggling with English or with structuring an argument
caught up fast. This is, I think, the strongest case for allowing these tools:
they are a patient, unlimited writing tutor available at 2 a.m.

## What broke badly

**I could no longer assign take-home code as assessment.**

A perfectly correct implementation of a neural network, submitted at 3 a.m.
with no comments and no version history, tells me nothing about who learned
what. So the work moved:

- Live lab tasks, timed and supervised
- Oral defence of submitted code — "change this layer and explain what happens"
- Reflection notes: *what did you try, what failed, what did you change*

Defence in particular is now my favourite assessment in the course. Students
who used a tool well can explain every choice. Students who didn't can't —
and the grade gap between them is honest.

**Facts needed verifying.**

Hallucinated citations remain common. Now every reference in a student
submission has to include the URL they actually opened, and I spot-check a few
per assignment. It's less about catching cheating than building the habit of
verifying.

## The rule that has held up all semester

> Use the tool freely. Own everything you submit.

If you can explain it, defend it, and modify it on the spot, it's yours —
regardless of who or what drafted the first version.

## What I'd change next semester

1. **Teach prompting as a first-week skill**, not an assumption. The gap
   between a vague prompt and a precise one is enormous, and nobody arrives
   knowing it.
2. **Grade the process.** Version histories and lab logs deserve more weight
   than polished final output.
3. **Set a "human-first" window** for high-stakes work — thinking before
   tools, every time.

The tools aren't going away, and I don't want them to. My job is to make sure
the assessment measures learning rather than typing speed. This semester got me
closer.
